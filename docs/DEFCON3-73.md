# DEFCON 3, October 1973

A brief and source audit for a force-posture study of the seventies: the worldwide alert of the night of 24 to 25 October 1973, the only one after Cuba, with the RUN button leading into the plan of the day.

## Thesis

SIOP//62 is a plan executed; Cuba is a ladder climbed; this is a posture held. Nobody came close to firing in October 1973, and that is the study's subject. The value of an alert with no war is that it shows the force as it actually stood: which bombers went to the pads, which boats sailed, how long each leg of the ladder took, and what the other side could see. The seventies force is the mature one, a thousand Minuteman silos, the first MIRVs, Poseidon replacing Polaris, and a Soviet ICBM force past numerical parity, and it was held under a plan, SIOP-4, that the government was three months from declaring too rigid to use. Nixon's NSDM-242 of January 1974 and Schlesinger's "limited nuclear options" were the answer; SIOP-5 followed in 1976. October 1973 is the last alert under the old doctrine and the case that made the new one.

The study has two halves and a second act.

- **The posture.** A DEFCON ladder as the readiness lab draws it, but from the record of one night: the WSAG meeting late on the 24th, the JCS message ordering DEFCON 3 worldwide, SAC's own steps, the 82nd Airborne and the carriers, the Sixth Fleet's movements, and the stand-down over the following days. Every step on the ladder is a documented message with a time.
- **The plan behind it.** RUN executes SIOP-4 with the forces the alert had generated, as the alert-force enactment does for 1961, against target categories rather than a transcribed list, because no seventies target list is in the record. The point of pressing it is the finding the seventies produced: assured destruction had no smaller option, which is why NSDM-242 was written.
- **Second act, October 1969.** Nixon's secret readiness test of 13 to 30 October 1969, the "madman" alert, with SAC bombers airborne over Alaska and the north Pacific for the first time since Chrome Dome ended. Same engine, no crisis, an alert as a signal; the contrast with 1973 is the decade's own story.

## What the study would show

- The ladder, drawn against the clock of one night: each message a step, each step with its time and its source, the readiness lab's option curve replaced by the DEFCON ladder as SAC and the fleet actually climbed it. Bombers move to the pads on the map; the carriers move on the Mediterranean; the 82nd stands to at Bragg. The Soviet side stays where it is, which is the finding.
- Pressing RUN: SIOP-4 with the generated force, allocated by the NUWEP categories as a stated rule, Soviet nuclear forces first. The Minuteman fields fire from six wings in the northern plains, the Soviet fields from the Volga to Transbaikal; the sum runs over the 1970 grid. The visible point is what assured destruction meant with seven thousand warheads and no smaller option, next to what SIOP//62 meant with three thousand.
- The 1969 act: the same ladder for the secret alert, with the airborne B-52s over Alaska and the north Pacific as tracks, and nothing on the other side of the map, because the alert's whole purpose was to be seen.

## What it must not claim

- No seventies target list is in the record. Targeting is by the documented categories and says so on every mark; the 1956 list has no part here.
- The number of Minuteman IIIs, boats at sea and bombers on the pads on the night are reconstructions from year tables and deployment patterns, tiered as such, until the SAC and Navy histories for October 1973 give them.
- The Soviet strategic posture is documented as unchanged; the study does not draw a Soviet response it cannot source.
- Casualty estimates for a 1973 exchange exist in the record only as the assured-destruction planning figures; the readout gives the engine's sum and those figures side by side.
