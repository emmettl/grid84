# Protect and Survive: Britain

A brief and source audit for a fallout study of one small island, from the Home Office's own exercises and the British scholarship that answered them, with what the Warsaw Pact's released plans say and do not say about Britain as a target.

## Thesis

Britain is the case where the fallout plume, not the fireball, is the subject, and where the state's own numbers and the scholars' numbers sit furthest apart. The Home Office ran Square Leg in September 1980 with 150 weapons and 280 megatons on the country and reported 8.5 million blast dead; Openshaw, Steadman and Greene put the same attack through a computer and got 29 million, more than half the population. Both figures are in the record. So is the 1955 Strath report, which found twenty-five years earlier that ten hydrogen bombs would kill twelve million and that fallout "presents problems of a revolutionary character," and was classified until 2002. The study is that disagreement on the map: the plumes drawn over real counties under the winds the Home Office assumed, the regional bunkers marked where they were, and the readout carrying the government's estimate and the scholars' side by side.

The Soviet side is the withheld tier made literal. The Warsaw Pact's 1979 Seven Days to the River Rhine, released by Poland in 2005, draws nuclear strikes across West Germany, the Low Countries, Denmark and Italy and leaves Britain and France unstruck, apparently because both could answer in kind. Whether Britain would have been spared in the general war that Square Leg assumed, or struck as Square Leg's planners assumed, is exactly the question the two sides' documents disagree on.

## What the study would show

- Square Leg's bomb plot as far as it was released, drawn as documented; the withheld inner-London part as a black bar over the capital, the study's first use of the withheld tier for a whole city.
- The plumes under the exercise's own wind assumptions, from the fallout lab's Glasstone contours, over the HYDE 1983 grid that is already prepared, with the ROC posts as the places the state would have read them from. The readout gives three numbers where the others give two: the Home Office estimate, Openshaw's, and the engine's own sum, with the difference explained by the shelter and evacuation assumptions each made.
- The regional headquarters as sites, with the bunkers that were themselves struck in the exercise marked so.
- Strath as a second act from 1955: ten 10 Mt weapons on the 1950 grid, the state's first look at the hydrogen bomb, with its twelve million against the engine's sum.
- The Soviet plan as a question: the Seven Days map with Britain blank, next to Square Leg's Britain, and the reasons each side had.

## Built, 9 September 2026

`#/study/britain-80` plays Square Leg's attack and `#/study/britain-80/strath` the ten bombs of 1955. The sites are built by [`scripts/build-britain-1980.py`](../scripts/build-britain-1980.py): 57 sites in the categories the record names, the airfields, naval bases, ports and command sites of 1980, all drawn withheld because the plot that may or may not have included them is; the seventeen regional government headquarters of the final configuration; and the 29 ROC group controls where the fallout would have been read. The bomb plot itself could not be had: the Ministry refused the requests of 2009 and 2011 and the written answer of December 1980 declined to name targets, so the weapons fall by a stated rule from the documented totals, Campbell's 69 ground and 62 air bursts, 1 Mt on the ground and 2 Mt in the air inside the documented range, the cities taken as the most populous cells of the 1983 grid with inner London left out as the exercise left it. The timing is the exercise's own, the first strike from 12:01 to 12:10 and the second drifting in from 13:00 to 15:00, and so is the southerly wind.

The two grids agree on the blast and disagree on the fallout by a factor of six, and the reason is the shape of a plume: tens of kilometres wide and hundreds long, it falls between HYDE's nine-kilometre cells and takes whole cells' people with it, while the 30-arc-second grid puts the people where they were. The finer grid is the one to read; the coarser column stays to show what resolution does to a fallout sum.

The engine brackets both estimates rather than choosing between them, and the readout says why: the Home Office assumed its shelters, Openshaw modelled the country without them, and the engine assumes none and sums every plume separately, so the people under more than one plume are counted more than once. The plumes themselves are the picture the Home Office did not publish: 57 ground bursts on a southerly wind put the whole island north of the Thames under fallout by the second day, with the regional headquarters at Hexham, Cultybraggan and Goosnargh sitting inside it.

**Strath** drops ten 10 Mt ground bursts at night on the ten most populous cells of 1950 from Long Range Aviation's fields. On the 1950 grid the engine reads 5.9 million dead by the blast-only method and 19 million with mass fire against the report's twelve million, which was blast, heat and radiation together; the report's own targets are not in the public summary.

## What it must not claim

- Square Leg's full bomb plot is not released. The study draws what was, marks what was not, and does not invent the rest.
- No Soviet target list for Britain is in the record. The SS-20 total is documented; its allocation is not, and the Pact's released plan is for a different war.
- The casualty disagreement is the subject, not a thing to resolve: the Home Office, Openshaw and the engine each made different assumptions about shelter, evacuation and fire, and the readout must say what each assumed.
- The JIC target papers must be read at the Archives or in Hennessy before any target is drawn from them.
