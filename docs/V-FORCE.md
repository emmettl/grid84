# The V-force

**A planning brief, 10 September 2026.** Proposed by the user: the RAF's V-bomber war plan as a study, and the question of how much of it was ever declassified. Not built. This note records what is on the record, what is not, and what the engine would need.

## Why it is the right case for flight profiles

The V-force is the arsenal whose story *is* its flight profile. It was designed for one thing, a high-altitude approach above the reach of guns, and within a decade the reason for its shape had gone. Gary Powers was shot down at 70,000 feet on 1 May 1960 by an SA-2, and the fleet that had been built to fly high spent the rest of its life on the deck, at 250 feet, in camouflage instead of anti-flash white. The Blue Steel missile, bought to let the bomber turn away a hundred miles out, was designed for release at 40,000 feet and had to be recertified for low-level launch, at which its range fell by most of what it had. No other force makes the argument of this instrument, that the weapon is what the delivery lets it be, quite so plainly. Grid/84 now has the machinery to draw it: air legs with a climb, a cruising altitude and a descent to the deck.

## What is on the record

Enough for an honest study, and less than one would like.

- **Humphrey Wynn, *RAF Nuclear Deterrent Forces* (HMSO, 1994).** The official history, written inside the Ministry of Defence with access to the files, and the single best source. It gives the readiness states, the dispersal scheme, the weapons and the shape of the planning.
- **The National Archives at Kew.** AIR 2, AIR 8, AIR 20, DEFE 4, DEFE 5, DEFE 6, DEFE 13 hold Bomber Command and Chiefs of Staff papers, many released, some retained. Individual operation orders have been opened in batches since the 1990s.
- **The Anglo-American co-ordination of 1958**, under which Bomber Command's targets were fitted to the American plan and later to the SIOP, and the Nassau agreement of December 1962 that assigned the Polaris force to NATO with the escape clause for supreme national interests. Both are documented, the second published.
- **What is public in outline**: quick reaction alert from 1962 with aircraft at fifteen minutes and later at cockpit readiness; the four-minute warning as the public expression of the same clock; dispersal from the main bases to satellite airfields in fours; the weapons, Blue Danube, Red Beard, Violet Club, Yellow Sun Mark 1 and 2, and Blue Steel; the transition to low level in 1963 and 1964; the handover of the deterrent to the Royal Navy on 30 June 1969.

## What is not

The target lists. British nuclear targeting has never been released in the way the SIOP-62 briefing was: individual desired ground zeros, the assignment of aircraft to cities, and the retaliation plan's contents remain withheld or heavily weeded, and secondary accounts of "the 48 cities" or similar figures differ from each other. **A study must not invent them.** The honest form is the same one the SIOP//62 study takes where the record stops: name the category, state the rule, mark the tier as withheld, and let the reader see the shape of the plan without a fabricated list. If the archive yields a released list, it can replace the rule.

## What it would need

- **An order of battle**, 1962 or 1964: Waddington, Scampton, Coningsby, Cottesmore, Finningley, Honington, Marham, Wittering and the rest, with squadron strengths from Wynn, and the dispersal airfields as a second layer.
- **Flight profiles**, which the engine now has: the high-level approach at 45,000 to 50,000 feet, then the same route flown at 250 feet, so the two can be run against each other and the difference in warning time and exposure read off.
- **Blue Steel**, which the standoff machinery already supports: about 100 nautical miles at high level, much less at low, released short of the target while the bomber turns away.
- **The warning clock**: the four minutes, the fifteen-minute readiness, the scramble, and the fact that the force's survival depended on being airborne before the first weapon arrived.
- **A restraint**: the readouts must say that the target list is withheld and that the geography of the strike is a stated rule, not a plan on record.

## The finding it would carry

That a deterrent can be obsolete in the middle of its own service life without anyone being able to say so out loud; that the answer to a surface-to-air missile was to fly under it, at a cost in range, accuracy and crew survival that was never fully argued in public; and that Britain's independent deterrent was, in its planning, fitted to somebody else's plan from 1958 onwards.
